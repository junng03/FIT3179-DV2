Australian YouTube creators · 2020–2026

Made for the feed

Australians now spend their screen time with people who film from their bedrooms, cars and backyards. This is the story of how the audience moved, who the creators are, where their fans live, what children watch, and how a new law redrew the line.

By Jun Hao Ng · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · October 2026

  • 91% of Australian adults used an online service to watch video in a given week in 20251

Part 1

The audience moved

Nine in ten Australian adults now watch video online every week.1 Paid streaming services such as Netflix are still the most popular way to watch, used by 68% of adults in 2025.2 But no single service is used by more people than YouTube: 70% of adults used it over six months, and 56% in the past week.1

The ranking below follows the eight most-used online video services each year. Follow the orange line to see YouTube overtake Netflix.

Short videos made by ordinary people are a big part of that shift, but the habit cooled in 2025. The share of adults watching user-generated or short-form video in a week fell from 59% to 52%, and the drop was steepest among the youngest adults.1

Part 2

Meet the creators

Who counts as an "Australian creator"? Ranking sites disagree, so this story uses one rule: an independent channel that lists Australia as its home country on YouTube. TV networks and other broadcasters are left out. The 40 largest such creators are shown below, one channel per creator.5,6

The map shows where they are based. Click a city or a bar to pick out that city's creators; double-click to clear.

Gaming dominates. Thirteen of the 40 creators mainly post gaming videos, and together they hold 41% of all the subscribers on this list. Not one of the 1,999 most recent videos from these 40 channels is marked "made for kids" on YouTube, a label that matters in Part 4.5

Creators now make two kinds of video: Shorts of a minute or so for the phone feed, and longer videos for the TV screen. Almost half (48%) of their recent uploads run three minutes or less. Gaming and science channels still make long videos, while comedy and lifestyle channels lean hard into Shorts.5

Part 3

Who's watching, and where

YouTube doesn't publish where a channel's viewers live. What we can see is where people search for a creator on YouTube. Google Trends scores each place from 0 to 100, where 100 is the place where that creator takes up the biggest share of searches. It shows relative interest, not the number of viewers.7

Four of the biggest creators are compared below. Their fans are spread right across the country, with the strongest interest often in the smaller states and territories: Tasmania for How Ridiculous and LazarBeam, and the ACT for Lachlan and Bridey Drake.7

Part 4

Made for kids?

Children are among YouTube's biggest audiences. A Roy Morgan survey of 1,129 children aged 6 to 13 found that 89% watch YouTube, about 2.5 million children. That includes 84% of 6–7 year olds and 91% of children aged 8 and over.8

What children watch changes as they grow up. Gaming is the top category at every age, watched by more than half of young viewers. Younger children add animation and animal videos; by 10 to 13, comedy moves into second place.8

Boys and girls watch differently. Boys drive gaming and sport. Girls lead on animals, music, fashion, DIY, unboxing and cooking. Comedy, animation and challenge videos, the MrBeast style, appeal to both almost equally.8

Part 5

The under-16 line

On 10 December 2025, Australia began enforcing a minimum age of 16 for social media accounts, and YouTube was included. By mid-December, about 4.7 million age-restricted accounts across platforms had been removed or restricted. eSafety's March 2026 update reported more than 310,000 further accounts blocked, while noting that many under-16s still had access.9

What under-16s lost on 10 December 2025

  • ✕ Their own YouTube account
  • ✕ Subscribing to channels
  • ✕ Liking and commenting
  • ✕ Posting their own videos
  • ✕ Supervised accounts and account-level parental settings

What they can still do

  • ✓ Watch YouTube while logged out
  • ✓ Use YouTube Kids, which is outside the law's scope

Logged-out viewing has none of the safeguards that apply to a signed-in child's account.

Sources: ChannelNews, December 202510; Roy Morgan, February 20268

Put together, two years changed what it means to be a creator in Australia: a new law, a smaller young audience on accounts, and a bigger role in how adults get their news.

About this visualisation

Why

Creators now compete with television for Australians' attention, and children are among their biggest audiences, yet most people never see the numbers behind that shift. This page brings them together in one story.

Who

Written for the average Australian. No statistics knowledge is needed. Percentages describe shares of people, not averages.

What

Survey data from the ACMA and Roy Morgan, channel and video data from the YouTube Data API, search interest from Google Trends, and official figures from eSafety. Surveys are used to compare platforms because views and followers are not equal units across platforms. YouTube does not publish creators' earnings or the ages of their viewers, so this page doesn't guess them.
