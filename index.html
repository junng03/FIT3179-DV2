Australian YouTube creators · 2020–2026

Made for the feed

Between 2020 and 2026, more of Australians' screen time went to people who film from their bedrooms, cars and backyards. This is the story of how the audience moved, who the creators are, where they're searched for most, what children watch, and how a new law redrew the line.

Part 1

The audience moved

In the ACMA's survey for the year to June 2025, nine in ten Australian adults watched video online in a typical week.1 Paid streaming services such as Netflix were the most popular way to watch, used by 68% of adults.2 But no single service reached more adults than YouTube: 70% had used it in the six months before they were surveyed, and 56% in the seven days before.1

The ranking below follows the eight most-used online video services each year. YouTube, in orange, overtook Netflix in 2023 and stayed on top through 2025, the latest survey year.

Not every kind of online video kept growing, though. Between the 2024 and 2025 surveys, the share of adults who had watched user-generated or short-form video in the previous seven days fell from 59% to 52%. The drop was steepest among the youngest adults: from 77% of 18–24 year olds to 62%.1

Part 2

Meet the creators

So who makes the videos? Ranking sites disagree about who counts as an "Australian creator", so this story uses one rule: an independent channel that lists Australia as its home country on YouTube. TV networks and other broadcasters are left out. That gives the 40 largest creators below, by subscribers on 10 October 2026, one channel per creator.4,5

Gaming dominated. On 10 October 2026, thirteen of the 40 mainly posted gaming videos, and together they held 41% of all the subscribers on this list, so gaming is the category picked out in orange.4

By October 2026, the way they made videos had split in two: Shorts of a minute or so for the phone feed, and longer videos for the TV screen. Of each creator's 50 most recent uploads as of 10 October 2026, almost half (48%) ran three minutes or less. Gaming and science channels mostly made long videos, typically 10 and 15 minutes. Vlog, animation and comedy channels leaned hard into Shorts.4

Short didn't mean more watched, though. By 17 October 2026, the longer videos had a median of about 285,000 views each, against 165,000 for Shorts. And not one of these 1,999 videos was marked "made for kids" on YouTube. That label matters in Part 4.4

Where do these creators work from? As of October 2026, only 12 of the 40 had a publicly documented home city, and eight of those 12 lived in Brisbane, Sydney or Melbourne. The exception was the biggest channel of all: How Ridiculous, based in Perth.4,5

Part 3

Where they're searched most

The creators live in a few big cities. Where are they searched for most? YouTube doesn't publish where a channel's viewers live, but Google Trends shows where people searched YouTube for a creator, here over the 12 months to 11 October 2026. It scores each place from 0 to 100, where 100 is the place where that creator took up the biggest share of searches. Because each score is a share of that place's own searching, a small state can top the list on far fewer searches than a big one: it shows where a creator is most popular relative to everything else people search for, not where most of their viewers are.6

Four of the biggest creators are compared below, and home was not where they were searched for most. How Ridiculous (Perth) and Lachlan (Brisbane) came second in their home states, and Bridey Drake (Central Coast) fifth in New South Wales. The top spot went to a small state or territory every time: Tasmania for How Ridiculous and LazarBeam, the ACT for Lachlan and Bridey Drake.6

Beyond Australia, all four creators followed the same pattern over those 12 months: Australia first, New Zealand second, then mostly the United Kingdom, Ireland, the Nordic countries and North America.6

Part 4

Made for kids?

Search data shows where creators are popular, but not who is watching. One audience that has been measured directly is children, and they were among YouTube's biggest. A Roy Morgan survey of 1,129 children aged 6 to 13, run between April and December 2025, found that 89% watched YouTube, about 2.5 million children. That included 84% of 6–7 year olds and 91% of children aged 8 and over.7

What children watched differed by age, but gaming stayed on top. In the 2025 survey it was the most-watched category at every age, watched by 53% of young viewers. It is also the category that dominated the top 40 creators in Part 2, none of whose 50 most recent videos (as of October 2026) were marked "made for kids". Children aged 6 to 9 also watched a lot of animation and animal videos; among 10 to 13 year olds, comedy came second.4,7

Boys and girls watched differently in 2025. Boys drove gaming and sport. Girls led on animals, music, fashion, DIY, unboxing and cooking. Comedy, animation and challenge videos, the MrBeast style, appealed to both almost equally.7

Part 5

The under-16 line

On 10 December 2025, Australia began enforcing a minimum age of 16 for social media accounts, and YouTube was included. By mid-December, about 4.7 million age-restricted accounts across platforms had been removed or restricted. eSafety's March 2026 update reported more than 310,000 further accounts blocked, while noting that many under-16s still had access.8

What under-16s lost on 10 December 2025

  • ✕ Their own YouTube account
  • ✕ Subscribing to channels
  • ✕ Liking and commenting
  • ✕ Posting their own videos
  • ✕ Supervised accounts and account-level parental settings

What they could still do, as at February 2026

  • ✓ Watch YouTube while logged out
  • ✓ Use YouTube Kids, which was outside the law's scope

Logged-out viewing had none of the safeguards that apply to a signed-in child's account.

Sources: ChannelNews, December 20259; Roy Morgan, February 20267

Put together, the two years from November 2024 to September 2026 changed what it meant to be a creator in Australia: a new law, fewer young viewers with accounts, and a bigger role in how adults get their news.3

The story in one line. Between 2020 and 2025, Australian adults moved to YouTube; by October 2026, its biggest local creators had answered with gaming and Shorts; they took the biggest share of searching in small places like Tasmania and the ACT, and in New Zealand; and in 2025, children loved the same gaming videos, none of them marked as made for kids. The under-16 law changed who could hold an account, not who could press play.

About this visualisation

Why

By 2025, creators were competing with television for Australians' attention, and children were among their biggest audiences, yet most people never see the numbers behind that shift. This page brings them together in one story.

Who

Written for the average Australian. No statistics knowledge is needed. Percentages describe shares of people, not averages.

What

Survey data from the ACMA and Roy Morgan, channel and video data from the YouTube Data API, search interest from Google Trends, and official figures from eSafety. Surveys are used to compare platforms because views and followers are not equal units across platforms. YouTube does not publish creators' earnings or the ages of their viewers, so this page doesn't guess them.
